What a privacy policy is for

What a privacy policy is for, what it has to explain, and why reading one is not the same as giving consent.

A privacy policy is an explanation of how an organisation handles personal data. In the UK it exists mainly because the law requires people to be told, in clear language, what is done with information about them.

It is information, not a magic spell

UK data protection law includes a right to be informed. A privacy policy is the usual way to meet that duty. It should say who is responsible, what information is used, why, who else receives it, how long it is kept, and what rights you have.

It is not, by itself:

  • a contract you accept by scrolling
  • consent to optional cookies
  • a promise that nothing will ever go wrong
  • a substitute for actually handling data lawfully

If a policy and the real service disagree, the real service is what the law cares about. The document cannot legalise a use it does not accurately describe.

What a useful policy actually covers

A short, honest policy is more useful than a long one copied from another site. The useful parts are usually:

  • the name and contact details of the organisation responsible
  • the kinds of personal data involved
  • the purposes and the lawful basis for each
  • who the data is shared with, including processors abroad
  • how long it is kept, in realistic terms
  • your rights, and how to use them
  • how to complain, including to the ICO

A policy that says “we may use your data for any legitimate business purpose” has not explained anything. A policy that lists every internal tool by name is not required either. The job is to make the processing understandable.

Reading it is not consent

You can be told about processing that does not need consent. Delivering a page you asked for, securing an account, or answering an email you sent often relies on other lawful bases.

Consent is a specific basis. It has to be a real choice, given for a specific use, and withdrawable. A sentence that says “by using this site you agree to this policy” does not turn the whole document into consent. Cookie pop-ups that bury “reject” are the same mistake in a different costume.

Some uses do need a genuine opt-in, especially some device storage. That is a cookie-consent question as well as a privacy-policy question.

What to do with one

Skim for the controller, the uses you did not expect, overseas transfers, and how to ask for your data. If you want a copy of what is held, that is a data subject access request, not a request to rewrite the policy.

This article is an explainer, not legal advice, and not any organisation's policy.
