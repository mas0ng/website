What a data subject access request is

What a data subject access request is, how the right of access works in the UK, and what to expect when you make one.

A data subject access request, often shortened to DSAR or SAR, is a request to see the personal data an organisation holds about you, and related information about how it is used. In UK law this is the right of access.

What you can ask for

You can ask an organisation to confirm whether it is processing personal data about you, to give you a copy, and to explain the purposes, recipients, retention, and sources where that is not already obvious.

You do not have to use the words “data subject access request”. A clear email that says you want a copy of your personal data is enough. You also do not have to use a special form, though a form can help the organisation find the right records.

The right is about your data. It is not a right to someone else's file, to internal passwords, or to every document that happens to mention a project you worked on.

How the process usually works

  1. You contact the organisation, ideally through the address in its privacy policy.
  2. It may need to check you are who you say you are. That is allowed, and it should not demand more identity evidence than the request reasonably needs.
  3. The normal deadline is one month. The organisation can extend it for complex or numerous requests if it tells you why.
  4. You should get the data in a usable form, plus the surrounding explanations the law requires.

In most ordinary cases you should not be charged. A manifestly unfounded or excessive request can be refused or made subject to a reasonable fee.

What you might not get

The right is real and it is not unlimited. Information can be withheld or redacted where another person's rights get in the way, where a legal exemption applies, or where the organisation is not actually the controller for that data.

A search also has to be reasonable. Asking for “everything you have ever touched that might relate to me” may still produce a serious search. It will not conjure records that were never kept.

If the organisation uses other companies to process data on its behalf, it still has to deal with your request. You should not have to chase every supplier yourself.

If you are unhappy

Ask the organisation to look again if something obvious is missing. If that fails, you can complain to the Information Commissioner's Office. You do not have to exhaust their process first, but a first reply often fixes simple mistakes faster than a regulator can.

This article is an explainer, not legal advice, and not a request form for any particular site.
