What Do Not Track does and does not do

What the Do Not Track signal was meant to do, why it failed in practice, and what browser tracking protection does instead.

Do Not Track, or DNT, is a browser setting that sends a header asking websites not to track you across sites. It is a request. It was never a lock.

What the signal actually is

When DNT is on, the browser includes a small piece of information with requests, conventionally DNT: 1, meaning the user prefers not to be tracked. Sites can read that header and decide what to do.

There was never a single, enforced meaning of “track”. Advertising groups, browsers, and privacy advocates did not agree a binding rule. Many sites ignored the header. Some treated it as a curiosity. A few honoured it for their own analytics.

So turning DNT on can make you feel as if you have opted out of tracking. In practice, most of the industry simply did not treat it as a control they had to obey.

Why it failed

A privacy signal only works if the other party has a reason to respect it: law, contract, or technical inability to do otherwise. DNT had none of those at scale.

It was also easy to lie about, easy to ignore, and easy to interpret as “please do not show personalised ads” rather than “please do not collect identifiers”. Without a shared definition, a site could claim compliance while still stitching visits together.

Browsers eventually stopped treating DNT as the main answer. Some removed the user-facing control. The header still exists in places, which is how a zombie setting survives in screenshots and old guides.

What actually reduced tracking

The controls that changed behaviour were technical and legal, not a polite header:

  • blocking or restricting third-party cookies
  • built-in tracking prevention, which stops many known trackers from running
  • intelligent tracking prevention and similar features that cut cross-site identifiers
  • cookie consent rules that require a real choice before non-essential device storage

A newer, related idea is a browser or device signal that some laws treat as an opt-out of sale or sharing, often discussed as Global Privacy Control. That is a different mechanism, with a clearer legal hook in some jurisdictions. It is still not a universal off switch for every tracker on every site.

What you should use instead

If the goal is less cross-site tracking:

  • use a browser with strong tracking prevention, or a reputable blocker
  • block third-party cookies if you can live with the broken embeds
  • reject non-essential cookies when a genuine choice is offered
  • treat private windows as a tidy session, not invisibility

Fingerprinting and pixels can still operate when DNT is on. A header cannot stop a file from being fetched or a canvas from being read.

Do Not Track was a good sentence. It was a weak control. Prefer the settings that change what the browser will actually send and load.
