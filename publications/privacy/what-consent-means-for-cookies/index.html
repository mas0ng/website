What consent means for cookies

What cookie consent actually requires, when a site can skip it, and why by-using-this-site is not a real choice.

Cookie consent is a real choice about storing or accessing information on your device. In the UK it comes mainly from the cookie rules, not from a privacy policy you have never opened.

Two different questions

What cookies are is a technology question. Consent is a legal one.

UK rules generally require clear information and consent before a site stores or reads information on your device, unless an exception applies. That covers cookies and similar technologies, including some pixels and local storage.

Separately, UK data protection law asks whether information about you is personal data, and which lawful basis covers using it. Consent can be a lawful basis there too, but it is not the default, and it is not the same as ticking “accept cookies”.

A privacy policy should explain both. It cannot replace the choice.

When consent is not needed

Some storage is allowed without consent when it is strictly necessary to provide a service you have asked for. Classic examples are a sign-in cookie that keeps a session working, or storage that is essential to security for that request.

“Necessary to make the site nicer” is not the same test. Analytics, advertising, extra personalisation, and most social tracking usually need a genuine opt-in if they store or access information on the device.

The exception is narrower than banners imply. A site that sets advertising cookies first and asks afterwards has already failed the sequence.

What valid consent looks like

If consent is the basis, it has to be:

  • Freely given. You can refuse without losing the service you actually asked for, unless the storage really is necessary for that service.
  • Specific. “All cookies” is a weak description. Advertising, analytics, and essential storage are different jobs.
  • Informed. You are told what is stored, why, and who is involved, in language a person can read.
  • Unambiguous. Silence, scrolling, or continued browsing is not a yes.
  • As easy to refuse as to accept. A giant Accept button next to a buried Reject path is not a fair choice.
  • Withdrawable. You can change your mind later without a scavenger hunt.

Pre-ticked boxes, “by using this site you agree”, and walls that make the site unusable unless you accept advertising cookies are the usual failures.

Consent is not a banner

A banner is just an interface. The legal act is the choice. A well-designed banner can record that choice. A badly designed one can pester you without obtaining consent at all.

Blocking cookies in the browser is a separate control. It can stop some storage even when you clicked accept. It can also break a sign-in that genuinely needed a cookie. Neither the banner nor the browser setting is a complete picture on its own.
