First-party vs third-party cookies

The difference between first-party and third-party cookies, why it matters for tracking, and what browsers now block.

A first-party cookie belongs to the site in the address bar. A third-party cookie belongs to a different site that has been embedded in the page. Same technology. Different reach.

The distinction in one picture

You visit news.example. That site can set a cookie for news.example. On a later visit to news.example, the browser may send it back. That is first-party.

The same page may also include an advert, a like button, or an analytics file from tracker.example. That other site can try to set its own cookie. If it succeeds, then when you later visit a different page that also embeds tracker.example, the tracker can get its cookie back. That is third-party.

The cookie does not have to contain your name. It has to be stable enough that the third party can recognise the browser across sites.

Why third-party cookies became the tracking tool

Advertising and analytics networks want to know that the person who saw an advert on one site is the same person who later visited another. A third-party cookie is a straightforward way to do that.

First-party cookies are more often used for the site you actually asked for: a sign-in session, a language setting, a basket. They can still be used for tracking on that one site. They do not, by themselves, follow you from shop to newspaper to forum.

For the basic mechanics, see what cookies are. For the legal choice about setting them, see what consent means for cookies.

What browsers now do

Most mainstream browsers now block or restrict third-party cookies by default, or offer a mode that does. That broke a lot of cross-site tracking that depended on them. It did not end tracking.

Workarounds include first-party cookies set via redirects, tracking pixels, browser fingerprinting, and other identifiers. Blocking third-party cookies is still worth doing. It is not a cloak of invisibility.

Some embedded features also break when third-party cookies are blocked: a widget that expected to stay signed in, or a payment frame that stored a session on another domain. That breakage is why “block all cookies” and “block third-party cookies” are different settings.

First-party is not automatically harmless

A first-party analytics cookie can still profile how you use that one site. A first-party advertising cookie can still feed a larger system if the site sends the identifier onwards.

The question to ask is not only “who set it?” It is “what is it for, who receives it, and did I have a real choice?” The party label is a starting point, not a verdict.
