What browser fingerprinting is

What browser fingerprinting is, how it differs from cookies, and why it is harder to see or clear.

Browser fingerprinting is a way of recognising a browser from the combination of details it already reveals, rather than from a stored cookie. No single detail has to be unique. The mix often is.

What a fingerprint is made from

A browser tells websites quite a lot in the ordinary course of rendering a page: its type and version, screen size, time zone, language, installed fonts, and how it draws graphics. Taken together, those values can form a fingerprint.

The point is not one exotic trick. It is that many ordinary, legitimate features leak slightly different information on slightly different machines. A site that collects enough of those differences may be able to say “this looks like the same browser as last time” without setting a cookie.

This article is about what that means for a reader. It is not a recipe for building a tracker.

How it differs from cookies

A cookie is something the site stores and later gets back. You can inspect cookies, block them, or delete them. The site has to ask the browser to keep that value.

A fingerprint is inferred from properties the browser exposes while doing normal work. Clearing cookies does not reset those properties. Using a private window may not either, if the same device and browser still look the same.

That is why fingerprinting is often discussed as a tracking method that survives “I deleted my cookies”. It is also why it is a poor fit for features you actually chose, such as staying signed in. A fingerprint is a guess, not a login.

How stable is it?

Fingerprints drift. A browser update, a new monitor, a font change, or a different network can alter the mix. Two people with the same laptop model can look similar. One person on a phone and a laptop will look like two browsers, because they are.

So fingerprinting is probabilistic. It can be good enough to stitch visits together for advertising or fraud detection. It is a weak way to prove that a particular human did a particular thing.

IP addresses sometimes get added to the mix. They change too, and they are shared. Combining signals makes the guess stronger. It does not make it a name.

What browsers and laws are doing about it

Modern browsers have spent years reducing the uniqueness of these signals: locking down what scripts can read, making more machines look alike, and blocking known tracking techniques. Results vary by browser and by version.

In the UK, storing or reading information on a device is also a separate legal question from “is this personal data?”. A fingerprint that identifies a person can be personal data even when no cookie was set. Cookie consent is about device access; a privacy policy is about what happens to personal data. They overlap here.

Private windows, tracker blockers, and “strict” browser modes reduce some fingerprinting. None of them make a machine invisible.
